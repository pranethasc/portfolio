Laser Cutting // Section 01

⚡ What is Laser Cutting?

Laser cutting is a manufacturing process that uses a high-powered laser beam to cut, engrave, or shape materials such as wood, acrylic, plastic, paper, and metal.

The laser beam is focused on a specific point of the material, producing intense heat that melts, burns, or vaporizes the material along a programmed path. This allows accurate and complex designs to be created with high precision.

Laser Cutting // Section 02

🔄 Stages of Laser Cutting

1
Design Preparation

Create the required design using CAD or graphic design software.

2
Material Selection

Select a suitable material and place it properly on the cutting bed.

3
Machine Setup

Set the laser power, speed, focus, and other required parameters.

4
Focusing & Alignment

Align and focus the laser beam correctly on the material.

5
Air Assist & Exhaust

Turn on the air-assist and exhaust systems.

6
Laser Cutting

Start the machine; the laser follows the programmed path and cuts the material.

7
Inspection

Check the cut for accuracy, quality, and completeness.

8
Cleaning & Shutdown

Remove the finished piece, clean the machine, and switch off the equipment safely.

Laser Cutting // Section 03

💡 Types of Laser Cutting & Processes

🔵

1. Diode Laser

Semiconductor

Uses semiconductor diodes and is commonly used for engraving and cutting wood, acrylic, leather, and other thin materials.

Best for: Thin materials & engraving
⚡

2. Fiber Laser

Fiber-Optic Medium

Uses a fiber-optic medium and is mainly used for precise cutting and engraving of metals such as steel, aluminium, and stainless steel.

Best for: Metals (Steel, Aluminium)
🔴

3. CO₂ Laser

Carbon Dioxide Gas Mixture

Uses a carbon dioxide gas mixture and is commonly used for cutting and engraving wood, acrylic, plastic, paper, and other non-metal materials.

Best for: Acrylic, Wood, Plastics, Paper

Laser Manufacturing Processes

✂️ 1. Laser Cutting

Cuts materials into required shapes with high accuracy.

🔗 2. Laser Welding

Joins two materials by melting them using a focused laser beam.

🎯 3. Laser Drilling

Produces small and precise holes in materials.

🖌️ 4. Laser Engraving

Removes or changes the surface to create designs, text, or patterns.

🏷️ 5. Laser Marking

Creates permanent marks such as numbers, logos, or barcodes on a material.

🛡️ 6. Surface Treatment

Modifies the surface properties of a material using laser energy.

Laser Cutting // Section 04

⚖️ Advantages & Limitations

✅ Advantages

  • High precision and accuracy.
  • Produces clean and smooth cuts.
  • Can cut complex shapes and designs.
  • Fast and efficient compared to many conventional methods.
  • Requires less physical contact with the material.
  • Produces less material wastage.
  • Easily controlled using CAD/CAM software.

⚠️ Disadvantages & Limitations

  • High initial cost of the laser cutting machine.
  • Requires skilled operation and proper setup.
  • Generates smoke and fumes, requiring an exhaust system.
  • Some materials, such as PVC and certain plastics, cannot be safely cut.
  • High power consumption for certain applications.
  • Maintenance of the laser, optics, chiller, and exhaust system is required.
  • Limited material thickness depending on the laser type and power.
  • Cutting thick or highly reflective materials can be difficult and slower.
Laser Cutting // Section 05

🛡️ Lab Safety & Safety Rules

👁️ Laser Safety
  • Never look directly at the laser beam.
  • Keep the machine cover closed during operation.
  • Do not disable safety interlocks.
  • Only trained users should operate the machine.
💨 Exhaust System
  • Switch on the exhaust before cutting.
  • Ensure smoke and fumes are properly removed.
  • Keep the exhaust duct clean and unobstructed.
❄️ Chiller Unit
  • Check the water level before operation.
  • Ensure the chiller is switched on and working properly.
  • Monitor the temperature and check for water leakage.
⚡ Earthing & Wiring
  • Ensure proper electrical earthing of the machine.
  • Check power cables and connections for damage.
  • Do not operate the machine with faulty wiring.
💨 Air Assist
  • Turn on the air assist during cutting.
  • Helps remove smoke and debris from the cutting area.
  • Reduces the risk of fire.
🧯 Machine Safety & E-Stop
  • Use only laser-safe and approved materials.
  • Keep the cutting bed clean and free from flammable materials.
  • Never leave the machine unattended while operating.
  • Keep a fire extinguisher nearby and use emergency stop if needed.

3D Printing // Section 01

🖨️ What is 3D Printing?

3D printing (Additive Manufacturing) is a manufacturing process that creates a three-dimensional object layer by layer directly from a digital CAD design.

The design is created using 3D modeling software, converted to numerical slices, and sent to the 3D printer. The printer deposits or solidifies material slice by slice until the complete physical geometry is formed.

PLA ABS PETG Resin (SLA/DLP) Nylon / SLS
3D Printing // Section 02

🔄 Stages of 3D Printing

1
Design

Create the 3D digital model using CAD or sculpt tools.

2
Slicing

Convert the model into printable horizontal layers & G-code.

3

4

3D Printing // Section 03

💡 Types of 3D Printing

🧵

1. FDM

Fused Deposition Modeling

Uses a heated nozzle to melt thermoplastic filament and deposits it layer by layer. Widely used for prototypes, models, and educational projects.

Technology: Material Extrusion (PLA, ABS)
💧

2. SLA

Stereolithography

Uses a UV laser beam to selectively cure liquid photopolymer resin layer by layer into solid plastic. Produces ultra-high detail and smooth parts.

Technology: Vat Photopolymerization
📽️

3. DLP

Digital Light Processing

Uses a digital projector screen to cure an entire layer of liquid resin simultaneously. Delivers high resolution and faster print speeds than point-by-point SLA.

Technology: Masked Projection Photopolymer
✨

4. SLS

Selective Laser Sintering

Uses a high-power laser to fuse powdered polymer (e.g. Nylon PA12) layer by layer. Creates strong, functional, and self-supporting complex parts without sacrificial supports.

Technology: Powder Bed Fusion (Nylon)
3D Printing // Section 04

⚙️ Manufacturing Process of 3D Printing

1. Digital Preparation (CAD & Slicing)

CAD Design: Create the 3D model in specialized design software.

Export & Slicing: Export as an STL/3MF file and slice it into layers to generate machine-readable G-code commands.

CAD / Slicer

2. Machine Setup

Load Material: Insert thermoplastic filament, liquid photopolymer resin, or metal/polymer powder.

Calibrate Platform: Level the build platform and preheat the print bed and nozzle to target operating temperatures.

Machine Calibration

3. Printing (Layer Deposition)

The machine builds the part slice-by-slice through FDM (extrusion), SLA (UV resin curing), SLS (polymer sintering), or SLM (metal melting).

Deposition Execution

4. Post-Processing

Part & Support Removal: Detach part from build plate; break off or dissolve support geometry.

Clean & Surface Finishing: Wash and UV-cure resin parts; sand, polish, vapor-smooth, or heat-treat for final quality.

Post-Processing
3D Printing // Section 05

⚖️ Advantages, Disadvantages & Limitations

🔍 Click to Enlarge
Figure 5.1: Key Trade-offs in Additive Manufacturing.

✅ Key Advantages of 3D Printing

  • Rapid Prototyping & Speed: Turn digital CAD models into physical parts in hours rather than weeks, accelerating product cycles.
  • Design Freedom & Complexity: Print intricate geometries, organic shapes, internal channels, and lattice structures impossible with CNC.
  • Zero Tooling (Low Initial Investment): No molds or dies required—extremely cost-effective for single parts and custom runs.
  • On-Demand & Customization: Easily personalize each item without extra setup cost (medical implants, dental, consumer products).
  • Minimal Material Waste: Additive layer-by-layer deposition uses only the required material, reducing scrap.
  • Supply Chain Efficiency: Digital inventory allows models to be stored in the cloud and printed locally on demand.

⚠️ Disadvantages & Limitations

  • High Cost at Scale: Inefficient and expensive for mass manufacturing compared to injection molding due to static unit costs.
  • Slow Production Speeds: Fabrication takes hours per part, creating bottlenecks for high-volume output.
  • Anisotropic Mechanical Weakness: Parts are built layer-by-layer, making them weaker along the vertical Z-axis bond lines.
  • Build Size Limitations: Physical chamber volume strictly caps maximum single-part dimensions.
  • Extensive Post-Processing: Requires manual support removal, sanding, chemical washing, or UV curing.
  • Restricted Material Selection: Fewer choices compared to traditional machining; specialized resins/powders are costly.

3D Printing // Section 09

🔍 Why the Object Cannot Be Made Subtractively

  • Complex Interlocking Clearances: The articulated model contains organic curved surfaces, internal mechanical clearances, and multiple moving sections that are designed to print pre-assembled in a single run.
  • Tool Access Constraints: Conventional subtractive processes (such as 3-axis/5-axis CNC milling or drilling) require cutting tool access. Rotating endmills cannot access enclosed, closely spaced, and hollow interior undercuts.
  • Assembly vs Single-Part: Producing this geometry subtractively would require machining dozens of tiny sub-components and manual assembly.
  • The Additive Advantage: Additive manufacturing builds geometry slice by slice, effortlessly forming internal moving joints and clearances directly from the digital code.
3D Printing // Section 10

📐 STL Definition & Triangular Mesh

STL stands for Stereolithography (or Standard Triangle Language). It is the universal file format serving as the bridge between 3D CAD modeling software and 3D printing slicers.

What STL Represents:

An STL file strictly represents the raw surface boundary (outer skin) of a 3D object:

  • Geometry Only: Defines where the outer shell exists in 3D Euclidean space.
  • No Metadata: Deliberately strips away parametric feature trees, color, texture, material properties, scale units, and internal assembly hierarchies.

Triangular Surface-Mesh (Tessellation):

STL approximates surfaces using interconnected 2D planar triangles (facets). Each facet is mathematically defined by the 3D coordinates (X, Y, Z) of its 3 corner vertices and a unit normal vector pointing outwards to distinguish outside from inside.

  • High-Density Mesh: Uses smaller, more numerous triangles for smooth organic curves (larger file size).
  • Low-Density Mesh: Uses fewer, larger triangles resulting in visible flat facets (smaller file size).
